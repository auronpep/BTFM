# Another Pass: From the Viewpoint of a 45-Year-Old Board Member of a $3M California Nonprofit

This changes the site.

This person is not looking for “What is a nonprofit board?” They are already on the board. They have sat through budget approvals, executive reports, fundraising updates, maybe an audit presentation, awkward compensation discussions, and meetings where no one knew whether the board actually approved anything.

For this user, the compelling site is not a beginner blog. It is a **boardroom field manual**.

The site should feel like:

> **“I have a board meeting next week. Help me understand what I should ask, what I should not meddle in, what documents matter, and where the real risk is.”**

That is much more compelling than “free nonprofit governance resources.”

# 3. The Big Insight: A $3M California Nonprofit Is Not a Small Charity

This should become a major content path.

At $3M, the organization likely has paid staff, contracts, insurance issues, donor restrictions, a public Form 990, professional fundraising concerns, and audit obligations. In California, charitable corporations with gross revenues of $2 million or more generally must prepare annual audited financial statements and maintain an audit committee, subject to the rules and exceptions in the Nonprofit Integrity Act materials. ([California DOJ AG Office][2])

That fact alone makes the site more urgent.

The IRS also states that organizations generally file the full Form 990 when gross receipts are at least $200,000 or total assets are at least $500,000, subject to applicable exceptions. A $3M organization is therefore in the world of public financial and governance disclosure, not just internal board habits. ([IRS][3])

## New mature-board message

> **Once a nonprofit has real revenue, staff, donors, contracts, and public filings, board service changes. The board is no longer just encouraging the mission. It is overseeing an institution.**

That sentence should guide the mature-board track.

```text

[ Executive director report ]
[ Compensation ]
[ Conflict of interest ]
[ Insurance / safety ]
[ Bylaws or policy changes ]
[ Difficult board member issue ]

```

Each option opens a meeting-prep page.

## Example: Budget approval prep page

```text
Before the meeting:
- Read the budget narrative, not just the spreadsheet.
- Compare the proposed budget to the strategic plan.
- Identify the ten largest changes from last year.
- Ask whether revenue assumptions are realistic.
- Ask what happens if revenue misses by 10%, 20%, or 30%.

At the meeting:
- Ask the CEO/CFO to explain major deviations.
- Ask what the budget authorizes officers to do.
- Ask what spending limits require board approval.

Minutes should show:
- The motion.
- The budget version approved.
- Any abstentions.
- Any follow-up assignments.
```

This is far more useful than a general article on “board financial oversight.”

The training outline already gives the right source material: budget planning should begin before the final meeting of the fiscal year, with general discussion first, final approval later, and deviation review after adoption.

# 10. Flagship Article Series for the $3M Board Member

## Series title

**Board Service After the Startup Stage**

### 1. **Your Board Is Not a Startup Board Anymore**

Core idea: once the organization has meaningful revenue, staff, contracts, and public filings, the board must shift from informal support to institutional oversight.

Tie-in from the book: the book proposes a board-growth model that starts with five directors and increases as annual revenue grows, topping out at 15 directors once revenue reaches $2 million. That should be framed as a provocative planning model, not a legal requirement.

### 2. **The Budget Is the Board’s Permission Slip**

Core idea: once approved, the budget guides and limits what officers, employees, and volunteers can do during the next year. This comes directly from the training notes.

### 3. **Trust the Executive Director. Verify the System.**

Core idea: the board should trust the executive, but trust is not a substitute for reports, proof, and corrective action.

### 4. **The Audit Committee Is Not the Finance Committee**

Core idea: a mature California board needs to understand the difference between financial planning and independent audit oversight. The California Attorney General’s audit summary states that the audit committee cannot include staff, the president/CEO, or treasurer/CFO, and imposes limits when finance committee members serve on the audit committee. ([California DOJ AG Office][2])

### 5. **The Form 990 Is a Public Governance Document**

Core idea: the board should know that the Form 990 is not merely an accountant’s filing. It is a public-facing governance and finance document. The IRS filing thresholds make this relevant to almost any $3M organization. ([IRS][3])

### 6. **Stop Using Board Meetings to Listen to Reports**

Core idea: the 40-40-20 rule should become a flagship article and tool. Reports should be read before the meeting; meeting time should be used for questions, engagement, and decisions.

### 7. **Minutes Are Evidence, Not Storytelling**

Core idea: minutes should show quorum, motions, decisions, proof of discussion, and reports, not become a transcript. The book and training materials both emphasize clarity over narrative detail.

### 8. **The Board Is Supreme. You Are Not.**

Core idea: the board acts collectively. Individual directors do not run the organization. This is one of the strongest truths from the book and should be repeated throughout the site.

### 9. **The Conflict Policy You Do Not Follow Is a Liability**

Core idea: policies are only useful if the board actually follows them. The book states that the only thing worse than having no conflict policy is having one the organization does not follow.

### 10. **Safety Is a Board Issue, Not Just an Operations Issue**

Core idea: safety includes financial safety, insurance, facilities, personnel, compliance, harassment, abuse prevention, youth protection, and reporting systems. The training materials and church syllabus both support this broader view of safety.

# 27. Tone Rules

## Do not write like this

```text
Nonprofit boards play an essential role in ensuring organizational success.
```

## Write like this

```text
A board that receives reports but never tests them is not governing. It is attending.
```

## Do not write like this

```text
Minutes should be concise and accurate.
```

## Write like this

```text
The minutes should let a future board know what was approved, not what everyone said.
```

## Do not write like this

```text
Boards should avoid micromanagement.
```

## Write like this

```text
The board decides direction. Officers execute. Individual directors do not freelance.
```

This tone matches the strength of the source material.

# 29. What Makes the Site Compelling

For this persona, the site becomes compelling when it does five things:

## 1. It respects that they are not a beginner

Do not explain everything from zero. Give them boardroom tools.

## 2. It helps them perform better at the next meeting

Every major page should improve a real meeting, vote, review, or board conversation.

## 3. It makes risk visible without being alarmist

Use red flags, checklists, and “when to call counsel” blocks.

## 4. It gives them better questions

The board member does not always need answers. Often, they need the right question.

## 5. It feels California-specific and lawyer-informed

Generic governance content is everywhere. A California nonprofit board site backed by nonprofit counsel is more valuable.

# 30. Revised Recommendation

The site should have two clear paths:

```text
Path 1: Starting a nonprofit
For founders, first boards, first meetings, fiscal sponsorship, formation, and tax exemption.

Path 2: Serving on a working board
For directors of established nonprofits with real revenue, staff, audits, risk, and public accountability.
```

The second path should become more prominent than previously planned.

The strongest version of the site is not a “blog and resource library.” It is:

> **A practical boardroom field manual for California nonprofit leaders who need to govern serious organizations without micromanaging them.**
