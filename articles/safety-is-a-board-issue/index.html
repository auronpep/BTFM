Safety Is a Board Issue

Safety belongs in board oversight when the risk could harm people, mission, assets, reputation, or legal standing.

The boardroom problem

The board does not run daily safety operations, but it must make sure serious risk is visible, assigned, insured, and reviewed.

The rule

Boards do not manage every safety protocol. They do need to know whether serious risks are identified, assigned, resourced, insured, and escalated.

The rule is that safety becomes board work when the risk could materially affect people, mission, assets, reputation, or legal standing. A board that never sees safety data cannot know whether the system is working.

Good oversight asks for patterns, not gossip. The board needs summaries, policies, coverage information, budget implications, and escalation paths. It should not turn itself into the investigator unless a specific governance role is required.

What the board should ask

  • What safety risks could materially affect people, mission, assets, or reputation?
  • Who owns this risk day to day, and what does the board receive?
  • What incidents or near misses changed since the last review?
  • Do insurance, policies, training, and reporting match the actual program?

What not to do

  • Do not vote only because a committee, officer, or executive says the issue is routine.
  • Do not let missing documents become a permanent board habit.
  • Do not use minutes to hide disagreement, conflicts, recusals, or uncertainty about the motion.

What documents matter

  • Insurance summary : Review coverage types, limits, exclusions, renewal dates, and known gaps.
  • Incident report summary : Show trends without unnecessary personal details.
  • Safety and reporting policies : Confirm who receives reports, who investigates, and when the board is notified.

What the minutes should show

  • The risk area reviewed and materials considered.
  • Any board direction, policy approval, budget allocation, or follow-up.
  • Any executive session or legal referral without unnecessary detail.

Red flags

  • The board has not reviewed insurance in years.
  • Incidents are handled informally with no trend reporting.
  • Programs involving vulnerable people have no board-level safety visibility.

When this becomes legal

Call counsel when incidents, mandatory reporting, youth or vulnerable-population safety, insurance coverage, employment discipline, investigations, or regulatory duties are involved.

  • A serious incident, allegation, or near miss has occurred.
  • Insurance may not cover a known program risk.
  • The board is unsure whether reporting, investigation, or corrective action is required.
