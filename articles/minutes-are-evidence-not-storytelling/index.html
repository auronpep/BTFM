The boardroom problem

Good minutes prove what the board did; they do not preserve every comment or smooth over weak process.

The rule

Minutes are not a newsletter, transcript, or public relations document. They are the board’s evidence that a meeting happened and that action was taken.

The rule is that minutes should be clear enough to prove the decision without recording every comment. Too much narrative can create confusion. Too little action detail can make it impossible to tell what passed.

Directors should review minutes as governance records. The question is not whether the minutes sound polished. The question is whether they show the meeting, the motion, the vote, the conflicts, and the materials the board relied on.

What the board should ask

  • Can a future reader tell exactly what the board approved?
  • Do the minutes identify recusals, abstentions, and conflict handling?
  • Do the minutes avoid unnecessary narrative while preserving the decision trail?
  • Are attachments or packet references clear enough to locate later?

What not to do

  • Do not vote only because a committee, officer, or executive says the issue is routine.
  • Do not let missing documents become a permanent board habit.
  • Do not use minutes to hide disagreement, conflicts, recusals, or uncertainty about the motion.

What documents matter

  • Draft minutes : Review for motions, vote results, recusals, abstentions, and packet references.
  • Approved agenda : Confirm meeting structure and action items.
  • Board packet archive : Preserve the materials directors relied on.

What the minutes should show

  • Date, meeting type, quorum, directors present, and directors absent.
  • Motions, seconds if used, vote result, recusals, and abstentions.
  • Materials reviewed and follow-up assigned.

Red flags

  • Minutes describe discussion but not the motion.
  • Minutes omit a conflict, recusal, or abstention.
  • Minutes are approved months late or not archived with packet materials.

When this becomes legal

Call counsel when minutes need correction, disputed actions, conflict records, corporate records demands, litigation risk, or regulatory review.

  • A board action is disputed or the minutes do not show approval.
  • Sensitive investigation, employment, compensation, or conflict issues appear in minutes.
  • The organization receives a records request or legal demand.
